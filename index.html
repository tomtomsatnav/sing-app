Singing practice for complete beginners

Your voice can learn to find the note.

Singing in tune is mostly a skill: your ear hears a note and your voice learns to land on it. That gets better with short, regular practice. Ten minutes a day is a good start.

      Short lessons · 2 minutes each

      How singing actually works.

      The basics that make the biggest difference for a new singer. Read one a day alongside your practice.

      1Tone deaf? Almost certainly not

      True tone deafness is rare. If you can hear that a familiar song is sung wrong, or tell a question from a statement by someone’s voice, your ear works. What most “can’t sing” people lack is coordination: the ear hears the note, but the voice hasn’t learned where to put it. That’s a motor skill, like learning to throw at a target, and it improves with feedback. That’s what the Pitch and Ear sections give you.

      2Posture: set up the instrument
      • Feet hip-width apart, knees soft, weight slightly forward.
      • Ribs comfortably open; don’t slump or puff your chest.
      • Chin level. Don’t reach up for high notes or tuck down for low ones.
      • Shoulders, jaw and tongue loose. Tension anywhere travels to the throat.

      Sitting is fine too: sit forward on the chair, back away from the backrest.

      3Breath support

      Singing is steady air turned into sound. Breathe low so your belly and lower ribs expand, not your shoulders. Then let the air out evenly, like the long “sss” in the warm-up. Uneven air is the main cause of wobbly, cracking or fading notes. Don’t over-fill: a comfortable breath is plenty.

      Try: hiss on “sss” and time it. Aim for 20 seconds of steady, even hiss. Many beginners start at around 10.

      4Open vowels make the sound

      You sing on vowels, not consonants. Drop your jaw a little, keep your tongue tip resting behind your bottom teeth, and feel space at the back of your mouth, like the start of a yawn. “Ah”, “oh” and “oo” are the easiest vowels to sing in tune. Narrow “ee” sounds tend to go sharp and tight at first.

      5Hum to find the buzz

      Hum on “mm” with lips lightly closed. You should feel a buzz on your lips and around your nose. That buzz is resonance, and it’s a sign the sound is placed forward rather than squeezed in your throat. Then open from “mm” into “mah” and try to keep the same buzzy feeling.

      6Matching a note: the method
      1. Listen to the whole note before you sing. Imagine singing it first.
      2. Hum it softly. Quiet sounds are easier to control.
      3. If you’re off, slide slowly up or down until it locks in. The dots turn green when you’re there.
      4. Hold it and notice how “right” feels. You’re training your body to remember it.
      5. Stay in your range. Notes near your speaking pitch are the easiest place to start.
      7Chest voice and head voice

      Low notes feel buzzy in your chest; high notes feel lighter, up in your head. The point where your voice “flips” between them is called the break. Everyone has one. Don’t push your chest voice higher to avoid it; lip-trill sirens teach your voice to cross the break smoothly over time.

      8Looking after your voice
      • Drink water through the day. Your vocal folds like being hydrated.
      • Always warm up, and stop if anything hurts or scratches.
      • Little and often: 10–20 minutes a day beats an hour on Sunday.
      • Avoid shouting, whispering a lot, or singing through a cold.
      • Hoarseness lasting more than three weeks is a reason to see your GP.

      Words you’ll see

      Pitch
      How high or low a note is.
      Semitone
      The smallest step on a piano, from one key to the very next.
      Cent
      A hundredth of a semitone. Within about 25–35 cents sounds in tune to most listeners.
      Sharp / flat
      Slightly too high / slightly too low.
      Range
      The notes you can sing comfortably, from lowest to highest.
      Interval
      The distance between two notes. Steps are small intervals; leaps are bigger.
      Break
      Where your voice shifts from chest voice to head voice.

      Train your ear.

      Your voice can only aim as accurately as your ear can hear. Listen to two notes and decide if the second is higher or lower. The gap shrinks as you get it right.

      About 3 minutes

      Warm up before you sing.

      A cold voice wobbles and strains. These three exercises wake up your breathing and ease your voice through its range.

      1. Breathe low

      Hand on your belly. It should move out as you breathe in; your shoulders stay still. Three rounds.

      Ready when you are

      2. Lip-trill sirens

      Close your lips loosely and blow so they flutter, like a child making a motorboat noise. Add voice, then follow the sliding tone up and down. If lip trills won’t come, hum or say “ng” instead.

      1. Lips relaxed; press lightly on your cheeks if it helps.
      2. Keep the air steady; don’t push.
      3. Slide smoothly; no jumps.

      3. Hum five notes

      The piano plays do-re-mi-fa-so-fa-mi-re-do, then pauses for you to hum it back. Each round starts a semitone higher.

      5 rounds, about 40 seconds
